Memory structure

Registers

  • Small blocks of memory located on the CPU itself.

  • Used to store intermediate values as the CPU works on them.

  • Very fast, direct access by the CPU with no latency.

  • Examples: IR, PC, accumulator.

    Modern CPUs often have severaal registers.

  • Fastest and most expensive type of memory

  • Very limited capacity – in the order of bytes.

Cache

  • Small, fast memory located claso to the CPU.

  • Intermediate storage between CPU and main memory:

    smaller and faster than main memory, slower and larger than registers.

  • Multiple levels of cache:

    L1 is the fastest, smallest and closest to the CPU.

  • Still expensive, therefore limited size – in the order of kilo to mega-bytes.

Main memory

  • Random access memory — each byte can be accessed with the same latency.

  • Very slow in comparison to cache.

  • Comparatively large capacity – in the order of giga-bytes.

  • Data and programs are loaded into memory from disk.

Volatile Memory

  • Registers, cache and main memory are volatile:

    data is lost when power is lost.

Disk

  • The slowest and largest type of memory.

    Much slower than main memory.

  • Persistent storage: data is preserved if power is lost.

  • Punched cards, then magnetic tape were used in early computers.

  • Later, magnetic spinning disks, hard disk drives (HDDs), were used.

  • Modern computers use flash memory: solid state drives (SSDs).

Network

  • Allows access to data on other computers.
  • Early machines used coaxial cable, later twisted pair Ethernet.
  • Modern computers use fibre-optic cable or radio waves Wi-Fi.
  • Orders of magnitude slower than disk.

  • Complex instruction set architecture (CISC), consumes around 20-80W of power.

    • Common to see 16GB in modern laptops.
    • DDR4 (dourbe data random access memory): high bandwidth with synchronous access for read and write, released in 2014.

  • SSDs are commonplace, unlikely to find a spinning HDD in a modern computer.

Analogy: let’s bake a cake

Imagine you’re plannning to bake a cake.

  • You have a limited number of bowls to weigh and mix ingredients,
  • A moderate amount of work surface to place items on,
  • and storage cupboards where large bags of ingredients are kept.
  • We will build a real-world analogy of computer memory by talking about making a cake.
  • Mixing bowls, spoons, measuring jug con be used to model CPU registers.
  • The work surface can be thought of as the cache – items can be easily accessed.
  • The storage cupboard can be veiwed as main memory, where items can be readily accessed, but more time and effort is needed to locate the item than simply picking it up from the work surface.

Load from memory into registers

  1. Fetch the butter from the fridge

    and sugar from the cupboard.

  2. Measure butter and sugar into a mixing bowl.

  3. Cream together butter and sugar.

  1. Fetching ingredients from cupboard / fridge is the same idea as loading data from main memory into the cache (work sorface).
  2. Measuring out ingredients is similar to loading values from cache into CPU registers (the mixing bowl).
  3. The process of creaming together butter and sugar is something like the CPU operating on its data in registers.

Add eggs

  1. Fetch eggs from the fridge…

    But you realise there are no eggs left.

  2. Fortunately, you keep chickens and they are laying well.

    Go to the end of the garden and collect some eggs.

  3. Returning from the shop, you place the eggs on the work surface.

  1. Rather than fetching from the comparatively quick main memory (cupboard / fridge), you have to do a “read from disk” – walk down the garden, go into the chicken coop and locate the eggs. This is comparatively much slower than retrieving something from the nearby cupboard.

Little aside on pre-fetching. - Had you known in advance there were no eggs in main memory, you could have optimised: - Ask your friend to collect eggs while you are mixing butter and sugar. You will be ready for the eggs, which they loaded into cache (put on the worktop), or better still, cracked straight into the mixing bowl as they return (load directly into registers), for your use.

Add flour and bake

  1. Fortunately, there is plenty of flour in the store cupboard.

  2. Fetch the sack of flour, weigh it into the mixing bowl and fold it into the mixture.

  3. Scoop the mixture into cake tins and bake in the oven.

7, 8. Similar to the butter and sugar situation, a relatively quick read from memory. 9. The oven is a bit like a co-processor. You can offload a particular task onto it and use the main CPU to do other things (you can prepare the butter cream / frosting while the cake bakes).

Cream and jam

  1. While the cake is cooling, you decide to whip the cream.

    There is no cream or jam in the fridge.

  2. You need to go to the shop.

  • This takes much longer even than fetching eggs from the garden.
  • So long that the cake is already cool.
  1. You return from the shop and place the cream and jam on the work surface.
  1. is analogous to fetching data from the network.
  • fetch from disk: go to the garden to retrieve eggs, stays “within the house” and while it takes much longer than going to the fridge / cupboard, it only takes a few minutes.
  • going to the shop, on the other hand, is much farther and takes much longer. it also requires a different protocol (choosing items, paying for items) than fetching something locally.
  • it also takes substantially longer.
